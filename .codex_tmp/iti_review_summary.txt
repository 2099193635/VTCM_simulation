[p:33] The Unique Characteristics of Railway Lines
[p:34] As a critical component of the nation’s transportation infrastructure, railway lines possess an irreplaceable uniqueness due to their operating environment and load characteristics:
[p:35] Long-distance, large-scale linear projects that traverse complex geological and climatic zones. The modern high-speed rail network spans a vast area, and individual train trips are lengthy. For example, the Lanzhou-Xinjiang Railway in China spans a total length of 2,423 kilometers, traversing extremely complex and diverse terrain—including the Gobi Desert and mountain gorges—and is constantly exposed to harsh weather conditions such as extreme temperature fluctuations and frequent sandstorms (Luan and Liu, 2025). This vast geographical and climatic diversity poses severe environmental challenges for the railway infrastructure.
[p:36] The direct medium for wheel-rail interaction, with extremely high safety sensitivity. Railway tracks are directly subjected to the immense dynamic forces generated by high-speed trains. Even the slightest defect in the track geometry or physical properties can be significantly amplified by the high-speed interaction between the wheels and the rails, which not only causes severe system vibrations but also poses a direct threat to train safety and passenger comfort. In the past few years, train derailments caused by severe track defects have occurred in many parts of the world (Ouyang et al., 2010; Kim and Yoon, 2013) , clearly demonstrating the critical safety implications of track defects.

It is influenced by local factors such as variations in subgrade settlement, structural discontinuities at switches and transition sections, and specific maintenance histories (Nabochenko et al., 2019; Wang, Liu and Euston, 2023a). Track geometric irregularities typically exhibit a high degree of spatial randomness and diverse short-wavelength discrete characteristics (Jin et al., 2025).

[p:63] General Survey
[p:64] In the context of modern railway infrastructure management, "census measurement" refers to the long-term, long-distance, and full-life-cycle inspection of the track network. This macroscopic evaluation is predominantly executed by dedicated Track Geometry Cars or Track Recording Vehicles, which provide continuous, high-speed, and non-destructive evaluations of the track's condition. While macroscopic census techniques provide essential network-level health screening, their architecture is not designed for localized, microscopic diagnostics. [p:67] Spatiotemporal Resolution Constraints. Running dedicated TRVs is operationally expensive and disruptive, forcing a constant conflict between safety inspections and commercial train services. These specialized vehicles require exclusive "track possessions," forcing infrastructure managers into a constant conflict between conducting safety inspections and maintaining profitable, regular commercial train services (Paixão, Fortunato and Calçada, 2019; Malekjafarian et al., 2023). Consequently, the frequency of network-wide census inspections is severely compressed. In reality, large-scale networks—such as the Dutch railway system—often limit these inspections to merely twice a year (Wang, Chang and Markine, 2018).
[p:68] This extremely low-frequency paradigm inherently restricts census data to isolated, short-term "static snapshots," creating massive temporal blind spots. Track degradation is rarely a perfectly slow, linear process; it frequently manifests as rapid track degradation or sudden damages induced by extreme dynamic loads, severe weather, or sudden material fatigue (Malekjafarian et al., 2023). Because census measurements leave months-long gaps between consecutive inspections, they are fundamentally incapable of continuously monitoring active dynamic damage or providing early warnings for these sudden, highly critical localized failures (Shi et al., 2018).
[p:69] Spatially, the macroscopic approach completely fails to support accurate full-life-cycle tracking of localized defects due to inherent positioning errors. Long-distance inspections rely heavily on mileage encoders and GPS, both of which are highly susceptible to wheel slip, route variations (e.g., changing lanes), and signal losses in tunnels or urban canyons (Wang et al., 2018). This vulnerability inevitably introduces cumulative "mileage drift" and spatial desynchronization across different batches of census data (Guo et al., 2025). Without absolute spatial synchronization, it is impossible to accurately evaluate the localized degradation rate of a specific defect over its lifecycle. Consequently, a defect recorded in one census might be misidentified as entirely new defect in the next, 
[p:70] Physical Sensing Disconnect. A critical load discrepancy exists between inspection vehicles (typically ~5 t axle load) and in-service trains (12–16 t). Standard inspection vehicles typically apply axle loads (e.g., 5 tonnes) that are significantly lighter than those of in-service passenger or heavy-haul freight trains (e.g., 12–16 tonnes). This severe load deficit means that structural defects highly sensitive to extreme dynamic loading—such as localized track stiffness degradation and dynamic settlement—remain unexcited and uncompressed during the census, leading to massive underestimations of the actual danger (OBrien et al., 2018).
[p:71] Furthermore, traditional census sensors primarily scan the surface geometry of the rail head. This "surface blindness" means the system only records the ultimate symptoms of degradation, remaining completely blind to the underlying structural root causes, such as localized subgrade settlement, poor drainage, or ballast fouling (Zarembski, Grissom and Euston, 2014; Wang, Liu and Euston, 2023b). This physical sensing blind spot becomes exceptionally catastrophic at critical structural nodes, such as bridge-to-subgrade transition zones and switches and crossings (S&C). In transition zones, abrupt variations in vertical stiffness generate extreme dynamic impacts that damage track components long before surface geometry thresholds are breached (Heydari, 2024). [p:72] Data Aggregation Flaws. As railway mileage expands, single-node computing architectures face severe processing bottlenecks, causing unacceptable delays in generating track quality reports for frontline maintenance (Guo et al., 2025). More critically, the data collected is overwhelmingly imbalanced; the vast majority of the track is healthy, meaning true localized defects are extremely "sparse" within the data ocean (Hou, Zhao and Yang, 2023; Kim, Fernández-Bobadilla and Chen, 2023). However, this macroscopic aggregation creates a dangerous "smoothing effect" that forcibly dilutes continuous defect boundaries and masks severe localized spot failures (Fontul et al., 2016; Sharma et al., 2018). [p:74] Reactive Maintenance Trap. The cumulative effect of the three limitations above traps census-based systems in a threshold-exceedance model: interventions are triggered only after safety limits are breached. Because census data lacks the capability to continuously capture subsurface causal parameters (like ballast fouling and thickness), it is fundamentally devoid of true "forecasting power" (Yurlov et al., 2019). Consequently, interventions are almost exclusively triggered after safety limits have been breached, forcing infrastructure managers into a reactive ("wait until it degrades and then fix it") rather than proactive maintenance paradigm(Zarembski, Grissom and Euston, 2014).
[p:75] Even when maintenance is initiated based on census alarms, the macroscopic nature of the data causes severe discrepancies during physical execution. Macroscopic maintenance planning often treats the track as a series of isolated points, completely ignoring the microscopic physical coupling constraints of adjacent track components, such as the stress linkage between adjacent fasteners (Sun et al., 2023b). Furthermore, blindly conducting localized interventions based purely on surface geometry alarms without addressing the underlying structural cause—such as aggressively tamping a section of track suffering from severe subgrade settlement—not only wastes immense resources but can mechanically accelerate track deterioration and ballast wear (Fontul et al., 2016).

[p:192] The Second Closed Loop: Precision Maintenance and Verified Intervention
[p:193] The second closed loop should be understood as a precision-maintenance loop rather than as a general robotics loop. In the proposed dual-loop architecture(Figure 2), the first loop supplies broad coverage: it observes long mileage, detects abnormal state evolution, ranks risk, and identifies candidate work sites. The second loop is activated only after such a candidate has been identified. Its purpose is not to inspect the whole network again, but to transform a warning into a local repair target, execute an intervention with controllable parameters, and verify the effect through remeasurement. This is the conceptual shift from “knowing that a section is deteriorating” to “knowing exactly what to repair, how to repair it, and whether the repair has changed the state as expected.”
[p:194] This definition also clarifies why the second loop should be evaluated differently from the first. The first loop is judged by coverage, frequency, sensitivity to state evolution, false-alarm control, and economic scalability. The second loop is judged by localization accuracy, diagnostic confidence, repair-boundary definition, parameter controllability, safety assurance, and measured repair effectiveness. A second-loop system that only adds high-resolution inspection is incomplete, because it does not close the maintenance action. Likewise, an autonomous machine that can perform a repair but cannot verify the resulting state is only an automated tool, not a closed-loop maintenance system. The defining property of the second loop is verified intervention.
[p:195] The planning view behind this loop is therefore deliberately engineering-oriented. It does not assume that general-purpose embodied intelligence can immediately replace railway maintenance crews. Instead, it treats intelligence as a layered capability embedded into a conservative safety regime. The lower layer provides precision localization, high-resolution measurement and tool feedback. The middle layer converts local state into repair parameters and acceptance criteria. The upper layer preserves human authorization, rule traceability and maintenance accountability. This arrangement is compatible with the railway context, where local autonomy is useful only when it remains auditable and recoverable under possession-window, traffic-safety and asset-management constraints.
[p:196] Four design principles follow. First, the loop must be target-specific: each task should define its own measurable state, action variable and acceptance criterion. Second, the loop must be coordinate-consistent: pre-repair measurement, execution and post-repair verification must refer to the same mileage and asset object. Third, the loop must be effect-oriented: success should be judged by state change and durability, not merely by work completion. Fourth, the loop must be incrementally deployable: early systems should focus on bounded tasks such as rail-profile correction, fastener intervention, local geometry restoration or ballast-condition verification before moving toward broader autonomous repair.
[p:197] From network alert to repairable engineering object
[p:198] The first step of the second loop is to convert a network-level alert into a repairable engineering object. A first-loop model may detect abnormal vibration, noise, track geometry, rail corrugation, stiffness variation, fastener anomaly or state-evolution rate over a relatively broad interval. Such an output is valuable for screening, but it is not yet a repair instruction. A repair instruction requires a much tighter definition: the target asset, the mileage position, the defect boundary, the dominant mechanism, the intervention type, the action parameters and the acceptance test. The second loop therefore begins at the point where condition monitoring becomes repair specification.
[p:199] This conversion requires spatial precision. A maintenance site must be mapped to a mileage coordinate, track, rail side or component identity. For linear assets, even a small coordinate mismatch can lead to ineffective repair or misleading verification. A geometry dip may have a boundary of only tens of metres; a corrugation problem may vary by wavelength band and rail side; a loose-fastener cluster may be discontinuous; a slab-support problem may be invisible on the surface but associated with a narrow support zone. The system must therefore express the defect not as an abstract risk score but as a geometric and component-level object that can be revisited.
[p:200] The conversion also requires diagnostic precision. Many railway defects are symptoms of deeper mechanisms. A local irregularity may result from ballast fouling, drainage failure, hanging sleepers, transition-zone stiffness mismatch, subgrade frost heave or slab-interface voiding. Repeatedly correcting the visible geometry without addressing the cause may improve short-term measurement values while increasing long-term recurrence. In the second loop, the target object should therefore include a causal hypothesis. The hypothesis need not be perfect, but it must be explicit enough to guide the choice between tamping, grinding, cleaning, tightening, replacement, grouting, drainage improvement or structural reinforcement.
[p:201] Finally, the conversion requires action precision. A repair category is not sufficient. Tamping requires lift and lining values; grinding requires depth, pass pattern and profile target; fastener intervention requires torque or replacement criteria; ballast cleaning requires depth and boundary; slab repair requires injection or local structural treatment parameters. The second loop becomes meaningful only when the system can connect the measured state to such controllable action variables. This is the main difference between precision maintenance and enhanced diagnosis.
[p:202] Precision measurement and local state representation
[p:203] Precision measurement is the local state factory of the second loop. It differs from the screening function of the first loop in scale, purpose and tolerance. Screening seeks high coverage and low marginal cost; precision measurement seeks repeatability, calibration and actionability. Its product should be a compact local state representation that a decision module, machine operator or robot can use directly. For railway line maintenance, this representation may include local geometry amplitude, spatial frequency, rail-profile deviation, corrugation depth, fastener status, ballast or support indicator, environmental condition, track-side identity, uncertainty and historical recurrence. The key requirement is that the state vector should be both physically meaningful and executable.
[p:204] A practical second-loop measurement system should combine three levels. The first is high-resolution surface measurement, including track geometry, rail profile, corrugation, surface defect and component-position sensing. This level defines the visible target and the required boundary of action. The second is support-condition or subsurface diagnosis, such as ballast fouling, voids, moisture or weak support zones. This level helps distinguish symptoms from causes. The third is dynamic or contextual measurement, including acceleration, strain, temperature, axle-load context and local response under operating conditions. This level is especially important at transition zones, where measured geometry alone may underestimate the mechanism that causes repeated deterioration.
[p:205] The central algorithmic problem is not only feature extraction but mileage alignment. The same target must be recoverable across the first-loop alert, local precision measurement, tool execution and post-repair remeasurement. This requires fusion of odometry, inertial sensing, GNSS or BeiDou when available, known track features, digital track maps, signal matching and machine-specific offsets. The issue is often treated as a preprocessing step, but in precision maintenance it is a core safety function. If before-after data cannot be proven to refer to the same object, the system cannot claim that the intervention changed the state.
[p:206] The local state representation should also include uncertainty. Railway maintenance is performed under time pressure, outdoor conditions and imperfect measurement. A precise-looking number without uncertainty can be misleading. For example, a predicted corrugation depth, ballast fouling index or slab-support anomaly should be accompanied by a confidence measure, data-source trace and recommended confirmation method. This makes the system more robust in deployment because it can distinguish between cases suitable for automatic action, cases requiring human confirmation, and cases requiring additional measurement before repair.
[p:207] Real-time decision-making and controllable intervention
[p:208] The decision module of the second loop should be local, real-time and intervention-oriented. Its role is not to generate an annual maintenance plan; that remains the task of the maintenance-management system and the first loop. The second-loop decision module answers immediate questions: what is the dominant maintenance object, what action should be taken, what parameters should be used, what risk should be controlled during execution, and what measured result will count as successful repair. This decision is narrower than network optimization but technically more demanding because it is tied to a physical operation.
[p:209] A robust local decision should combine four sources of reasoning. Standards and management rules define the safety envelope and acceptance thresholds. Expert rules encode the causal knowledge of track engineers, such as when repeated tamping is unlikely to solve a transition-zone problem. Predictive models estimate recurrence and intervention durability. Local measurement provides the immediate state and action boundary. The result should not be a black-box command. It should be an auditable recommendation that includes the state evidence, confidence level, selected action, process parameters, acceptance criterion and fallback action. In safety-critical contexts, the system should be allowed to recommend human review instead of forced autonomy.
[p:210] The action model should be expressed as a state-action-effect tuple. The state contains the local features and their uncertainty; the action contains the repair type and process parameters; the effect contains the target before-after change and the expected durability. This tuple is useful for two reasons. Operationally, it tells the machine or crew what to do and how to verify it. Scientifically, it creates a rare but valuable dataset: pre-repair state, executed action, process log, post-repair state and later recurrence. Such paired intervention data are more useful for future learning than isolated inspection data because they record how the infrastructure responds to maintenance.
[p:211] Controllable intervention requires process feedback. For grinding, this may include actual removal depth, post-profile error and thermal or surface-quality constraints. For tamping, it may include lift, lining, compaction and residual geometry deviation. For fastener work, it may include target localization, torque, tool angle and visual confirmation. For ballast or slab support treatment, it may include injected volume, pressure response, subsurface change and local geometry recovery. The common requirement is that the machine should not only receive a command; it should report what was actually delivered. This delivery record is the bridge between decision and verification.
[p:212] The second loop also changes the meaning of optimization. In conventional planning, optimization often means minimizing cost, possession time or expected risk at the network level. In precision maintenance, optimization is local and effect-based: choose the smallest safe intervention that changes the state sufficiently and durably. This principle is important because over-intervention may waste possession time and damage materials, while under-intervention may produce rapid recurrence. The second loop should therefore learn not only where to intervene, but also how much intervention is enough.
[p:213] Robotic execution, verification feedback and deployment roadmap
[p:214] Robotic execution is a necessary enabler for the mature second loop, but it should not dominate the concept. The immediate goal is not a fully general railway repair robot. The credible path is task-constrained automation: autonomous or remotely supervised mobility, high-precision localization, specialized sensing payloads, constrained tool modules, process monitoring, human authorization and post-work verification. Existing autonomous railway platforms already show that localization, obstacle detection, remote monitoring and modular payload integration can be achieved in railway environments (Li et al., 2024). These platforms can become carriers for second-loop measurement and intervention modules, provided that the repair task is well bounded and the safety case is explicit.
[p:215] The same incremental logic applies to intervention tools. Fastener automation is a useful example because the target is discrete, the operation is bounded, and the endpoint can be measured by torque and visual confirmation. The rail-fastener installation robot developed by Guo combines visual recognition, bolt localization, manipulator feeding and electric-wrench fastening. The broader significance of such a system is not that it solves all maintenance tasks, but that it demonstrates the structure of a feasible precision-repair module: identify the component, localize the target, execute a controlled action, and verify the endpoint. After the intervention, the system must measure the same target again using the same coordinate reference or a calibrated equivalent. The remeasurement should answer four questions: was the target reached, did the state change as intended, does the residual state satisfy the acceptance criterion, and should another local correction be performed? This makes maintenance evidence-based at the action level. A conventional work record may show that a grinding, tamping or fastening job was completed. A closed-loop record shows the pre-repair state, the selected action, the delivered process parameters, the post-repair state and the decision about acceptance or rework.

The third stage is semi-automated execution, where specialized machines or robots execute bounded operations under human supervision and report process data. The fourth stage is closed-loop learning, where repeated state-action-effect records are used to improve local decisions, quantify repair durability and update the first-loop state-evolution models. The objective is no longer only to collect more data, but to make knowledge learned from one operating domain usable in another. Domain adaptation partially reduces the distribution gap between lines, yet it does not remove the need for engineering validation. Changes in defect definitions, maintenance standards, sensor layouts and vehicle-track interaction can still produce incorrect transfer. [p:232] At the asset scale, Milosevic et al. (2022) combined sleeper-mounted accelerometers, three-dimensional geometry scans and multi-body vehicle-track simulations for six in situ crossing panels. The proposed condition indicator was calculated from track responses in a wavelength-related frequency band and exhibited a strong relationship with simulated vertical wheel-rail contact force under different crossing and track conditions. This case is significant because it links a measurable dynamic response to a physically meaningful interaction quantity. It therefore provides more diagnostic value than an isolated acceleration threshold.
[p:233] The measured-simulated comparison also exposes the limitations of the current implementation. The scanned crossing regions were shorter than the full dynamic interaction zone, and measured accelerations contained excitation sources not represented in the model, including long-wavelength irregularities, wheel out-of-roundness and conditions outside the scanned area. Calibration was more successful for sleeper displacement than for velocity and acceleration. These findings are not weaknesses to be hidden; they illustrate the role of open-loop uncertainty in real railway applications. A physics-assisted monitoring system must continuously update its boundary conditions and quantify the discrepancy between the model and the measured asset before its indicator can be used to trigger maintenance
[p:234] The studies by Rahman et al. (2024), Ghiasi et al. (2025) and Milosevic et al. (2022) can thus be read as a connected sequence. DAS expands temporal and spatial coverage, drive-by monitoring uses operational vehicles to acquire repeated line-scale responses, domain adaptation supports knowledge transfer between lines, and asset-specific sensing supplies local diagnostic resolution. Together they define a multi-scale perception architecture. The unresolved issue is how to coordinate these levels so that a weak corridor-scale anomaly can initiate targeted inspection, obtain a mechanism-oriented diagnosis and produce an executable work order.
[p:235] From Geometric Exceedance to Condition Interpretation and State Evolution
[p:236] After perception, the next requirement is to transform measured abnormalities into infrastructure states. Conventional geometry inspection is highly effective for identifying where alignment, level, gauge or twist exceed specified limits. Nevertheless, the measured geometry is the combined consequence of the rail, fasteners, sleepers, ballast, subgrade, drainage, environmental exposure and maintenance history. Intelligent assessment must therefore progress from threshold exceedance to component inference, hidden-state diagnosis and evolution modelling.
[p:237] Sresakoolchai and Kaewunruen (2022) demonstrated the first stage of this progression by combining track geometry measurements, component-defect records and track-profile data. Their case covered a 30-km section measured at one-foot intervals over four years from 2016 to 2019. Supervised models were used to identify rail, rail-joint, fastener and switch-and-crossing defects, while clustering and association-rule analysis were used to explore relationships among defect types and track characteristics. The best-performing deep neural network achieved approximately 94% accuracy. [p:238] This form of data reuse can increase the value of existing inspection systems without requiring immediate replacement of the sensing platform. However, it also shifts the bottleneck toward data governance. Component-defect labels must be spatially synchronized with geometry records, and differences in inspection timing can introduce false associations. Rare but safety-critical defects may be underrepresented, while high aggregate accuracy can conceal poor sensitivity for individual classes. The model output should therefore be interpreted as diagnostic evidence for targeted reinspection rather than as an autonomous maintenance command.
[p:239] Wang, Liu and Euston (2023) extended geometry-based interpretation further by integrating track geometry defects with substructure and operational information on a passenger railroad in the United States. Their framework combined GPR-derived ballast-condition variables, rail-seat abrasion, traffic density, track class, curvature and maintenance-related information to estimate the risk of geometry-defect occurrence. This case changes the diagnostic question from "which component defect accompanies the measured geometry?" to "which underlying support and operating conditions make geometry defects more likely?" It therefore represents a transition from component classification to hidden-state and risk interpretation.

[p:242] The integration framework in Wang et al. is particularly relevant to the proposed first loop because it shows that data fusion is not merely the concatenation of sensor channels. Geometry, GPR, rail-seat abrasion, traffic and track-class data possess different spatial resolutions and reference systems. They must be segmented, registered and converted into consistent features before a data-driven risk model can be established. The case thus provides an engineering counterpart to the multi-source fusion concept discussed earlier in this paper.
[p:243] The residual limitation is causal ambiguity. A geometry defect may result from ballast fouling, local stiffness variation, rail-seat abrasion, drainage failure or interacting causes. A predictive model can identify statistical risk without proving a unique deterioration mechanism. Consequently, its output should be used to guide additional inspection and maintenance prioritization. The precision intervention loop requires one further step: converting a high-risk section into a justified treatment type, such as tamping, ballast cleaning, drainage improvement, sleeper treatment or subgrade reinforcement.
[p:244] Environmental cases broaden the concept of hidden state from structural condition to external forcing. Roghani (2021) investigated a frost-susceptible track section over the winter-spring periods of 2019 and 2020 using thermistors, total-station surveys and track geometry measurements. The observations linked freezing-degree days, frost penetration, track-surface deformation and changes in profile and alignment roughness. The study showed that freeze-thaw cycling can alter both the magnitude and spatial distribution of track deformation, particularly near geotechnically sensitive features such as culverts.

[p:247] This field evidence has two implications for state-evolution modelling. First, deterioration cannot always be represented by a stationary curve derived only from historical geometry measurements. The evolution law changes when moisture, frost depth and temperature cycles change. Second, prediction confidence should vary with environmental conditions. During a period of rapid thaw or exceptional temperature fluctuation, a model trained on ordinary operating data should express greater uncertainty and trigger denser observation.
[p:248] Kampczyk and Dybeł (2021) provide a related asset-scale example through their turnout-oriented digital-twin foundation. Their monitoring station integrated an external sensor into an S49 rail and recorded rail-head temperature together with ambient temperature, humidity and light. Although the implementation does not yet constitute a complete operational digital twin, it demonstrates that environmental boundary conditions must form part of the asset-state representation. A turnout twin based only on nominal geometry would omit variables capable of explaining thermal expansion, measurement variation and weather-dependent deterioration.
[p:249] The above cases form a coherent assessment chain. Geometry data can first be used to infer component defects, then integrated with substructure and operational variables to estimate deterioration risk, and finally interpreted within changing environmental boundary conditions. This progression is central to the "state evolution" philosophy: the objective is not only to describe the current track geometry, but to determine which mechanism is active, how confidently it has been identified and how the condition may evolve under future traffic and environmental loading.
[p:250] From Predictive Decision-Making to Physical Intervention and Feedback
[p:251] The output of state assessment has operational value only when it changes maintenance action. This requires a transition from diagnosis to timing, treatment selection and resource allocation. It also requires post-intervention monitoring, because an intelligent O&M system must learn whether the selected treatment changed the subsequent deterioration trajectory.
[p:252] Praticò and Fedele (2025) examined the economic logic of railway predictive maintenance by comparing renewal, reactive maintenance and proactive maintenance under different degradation curves and sensing-system cost scenarios. Their analysis emphasizes that a higher intervention frequency is not automatically preferable. The optimal strategy depends on the shape of the degradation curve, the condition level at intervention, the resulting recovery and the cost of sensing and maintenance. The study therefore supplies an important decision layer between condition prediction and physical execution.
[p:254] Figure (a) Railway track condition as a function of maintenance strategy and Age of the Track Asset (ATA) ; (b) main types of degradation curves (Key Performance Indicator, KPI, vs. Age of the track Asset, ATA): (c) overall view, (d) without maintenance, (e) with reactive maintenance, and (d) with proactive maintenance(Praticò and Fedele, 2025)
[p:255] Within the first loop, the contribution of this analysis is to convert "earlier detection" into a constrained maintenance decision. Continuous sensing has a cost, false alarms create unnecessary intervention, and premature maintenance can consume resources without producing proportional performance gains. Conversely, delayed intervention can accelerate deterioration and increase renewal costs. Predictive maintenance should therefore optimize the expected long-term outcome rather than minimize a single condition indicator. In practical deployment, the economic model must be connected with line availability, maintenance-window constraints, safety risk and the measured effectiveness of alternative treatments.
[p:256] Fischer (2022) provides field evidence for the intervention and feedback side of the architecture. The study examined geogrid reinforcement on a test section of Hungarian mainline No. 1 between Kelenföld and the Hegyeshalom state border. The site was established in 2010, and its geometry was monitored through rail-head levelling from 2010 to 2014 and recording-car measurements from 2010 to 2021. Different geogrid and control subsections enabled the long-term comparison of geometry deterioration under accumulated traffic and repeated maintenance.
[p:257] This case is more than an example of a reinforcement material. In the dual-loop context, it illustrates how a physical intervention can be evaluated by its influence on the future state trajectory. The maintenance action is not treated as the end of the process. Instead, subsequent geometry measurements are used to compare deterioration phases, traffic accumulation and the performance of treated and untreated sections. This is the type of feedback required to update deterioration models and future treatment selection.
[p:258] The field evidence also demonstrates why intervention assessment is difficult. Drainage condition, water pockets, precipitation, ballast screening, traffic accumulation and repeated tamping all influence the measured geometry. A simple before-and-after comparison may therefore attribute too much of the observed change to the intervention itself. A closed-loop system should maintain a structured record of treatment type, location, date, environmental condition, traffic exposure and post-maintenance response. Such records would allow the maintenance model to distinguish immediate geometry recovery from long-term deterioration reduction.
[p:259] Praticò and Fedele (2025) and Fischer (2022) together connect the two sides of the second loop. The former asks when predictive maintenance is economically justified, whereas the latter shows how the long-term effect of a physical intervention can be measured. The remaining integration task is to connect predicted risk and cost-benefit analysis to a specific executable treatment and then return the measured outcome to the model. Without this feedback, predictive maintenance remains a planning concept rather than a learning closed loop.

[p:67] Spatiotemporal Resolution Constraints. Running dedicated TRVs is operationally expensive and disruptive, forcing a constant conflict between safety inspections and commercial train services. This "surface blindness" means the system only records the ultimate symptoms of degradation, remaining completely blind to the underlying structural root causes, such as localized subgrade settlement, poor drainage, or ballast fouling (Zarembski, Grissom and Euston, 2014; Wang, Liu and Euston, 2023b). This physical sensing blind spot becomes exceptionally catastrophic at critical structural nodes, such as bridge-to-subgrade transition zones and switches and crossings (S&C). In transition zones, abrupt variations in vertical stiffness generate extreme dynamic impacts that damage track components long before surface geometry thresholds are breached (Heydari, 2024). Similarly, at complex S&C nodes, the inherent geometric discontinuities (physical gaps in the rail) not only render conventional automated sensors (e.g., eddy current or image recognition) ineffective, but also completely confound dynamic sensors, which struggle to separate normal structural gap impacts from actual defect-induced vibrations (Wei et al., 2017). [p:72] Data Aggregation Flaws. High data volume does not equate to diagnostic accuracy: true defects are extremely sparse within the data, and macroscopic aggregation into indices such as TQI introduces a smoothing effect that masks localized failures. As railway mileage expands, single-node computing architectures face severe processing bottlenecks, causing unacceptable delays in generating track quality reports for frontline maintenance (Guo et al., 2025). More critically, the data collected is overwhelmingly imbalanced; the vast majority of the track is healthy, meaning true localized defects are extremely "sparse" within the data ocean (Hou, Zhao and Yang, 2023; Kim, Fernández-Bobadilla and Chen, 2023). This extreme sparsity, coupled with the sensors' vulnerability to environmental noise (e.g., sunlight, rain, electromagnetic interference) and the lack of precise physical labels, heavily degrades the reliability of automated machine learning classifiers, often necessitating slow and highly subjective expert interpretation (Hou, Zhao and Yang, 2023; Guo et al., 2025).
[p:73] To manage this data explosion, industry practices mathematically aggregate track measurements into macroscopic indicators, such as the Track Quality Index (TQI), over fixed spatial segments (e.g., 200 meters). However, this macroscopic aggregation creates a dangerous "smoothing effect" that forcibly dilutes continuous defect boundaries and masks severe localized spot failures (Fontul et al., 2016; Sharma et al., 2018). Furthermore, census systems generally evaluate defects of different wavelengths (e.g., long-wave track settlement and short-wave dipped joints) in total isolation. The widespread use of chord measurement methods further exacerbates this by introducing Track Irregularity Conversion Errors (TICE), where the same physical defect yields entirely different severity scores depending on the chord's starting position (OBrien et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2023b). [p:74] Reactive Maintenance Trap. The cumulative effect of the three limitations above traps census-based systems in a threshold-exceedance model: interventions are triggered only after safety limits are breached. Because census data lacks the capability to continuously capture subsurface causal parameters (like ballast fouling and thickness), it is fundamentally devoid of true "forecasting power" (Yurlov et al., 2019). Consequently, interventions are almost exclusively triggered after safety limits have been breached, forcing infrastructure managers into a reactive ("wait until it degrades and then fix it") rather than proactive maintenance paradigm(Zarembski, Grissom and Euston, 2014).
[p:75] Even when maintenance is initiated based on census alarms, the macroscopic nature of the data causes severe discrepancies during physical execution. Macroscopic maintenance planning often treats the track as a series of isolated points, completely ignoring the microscopic physical coupling constraints of adjacent track components, such as the stress linkage between adjacent fasteners (Sun et al., 2023b). Furthermore, blindly conducting localized interventions based purely on surface geometry alarms without addressing the underlying structural cause—such as aggressively tamping a section of track suffering from severe subgrade settlement—not only wastes immense resources but can mechanically accelerate track deterioration and ballast wear (Fontul et al., 2016).

[p:81] Railways are the backbone of modern transportation systems, and the integrity of their infrastructure directly affects operational safety and transportation efficiency. According to statistics, infrastructure failures—including broken rails, ballast settlement, and bridge damage—are among the leading causes of railway accidents(Bianchi et al., 2025a). Traditional periodic manual inspections are no longer sufficient to meet the inspection needs of modern high-density, high-speed rail networks; as a result, automated, continuous, and intelligent condition assessment technologies have become a hot topic of research. The scope of railway condition assessment encompasses track structures (rails, fasteners, sleepers, ballast, and subgrade), bridge and tunnel structures, as well as functional equipment such as overhead contact systems and turnouts. The objectives of such assessments can be categorized into three areas: defect detection, condition grading, and degradation trend prediction, which correspond to the three maintenance strategies of reactive repair, condition-based maintenance, and predictive maintenance, respectively(Bris-Peñalver, Verdecia-Peña and Alonso, 2026a).
[p:82] As we mentioned in the Introduction part before, several systematic reviews have provided an overview of the current state of the field in recent years. Bianchi et al.(2025b) provided a comprehensive classification of monitoring technologies ranging from classical to modern methods; Khajehdezfuly, Azizipour and Kaewunruen (2025) have reviewed more than 500 studies on AI applications and systematically described the current status of AI technology adoption across various components and fields within the railway sector. Building on the aforementioned work, this section constructs a comprehensive review framework spanning three tiers—traditional, cutting-edge, and AI—with a focus on methodological dimensions, supplemented by technological maturity and application scenarios.
[p:84] The core task of evaluating track geometry inspection data is to convert multidimensional measurements into quantifiable condition ratings to support maintenance decisions. Among the most widely used metrics are the Track Quality Index (TQI) and the Track Geometry Index (TGI), both of which are quantitative indicators that assess the condition of railway tracks by evaluating multiple geometric parameters (Safari Tarbozagh et al., 2026). The core significance of these two types of metrics lies in condensing the geometric dispersion of spatial distribution into a single management metric, thereby enabling the prioritized allocation of maintenance resources.
[p:85] However, the degradation of the geometric condition is also related to changes in the support conditions of the ballast and subgrade; relying solely on the TQI or TGI makes it difficult to distinguish between different degradation mechanisms. Morais et al. (2023) proposed a method for assessing the condition of railway track supports based on modal analysis of the natural frequencies of multi-unit systems. This approach utilizes tools for monitoring and analyzing the natural frequencies of specific sections of the railway track to obtain information regarding the condition of the track support system—particularly the subgrade. By incorporating tensor decomposition analysis, Woldemariam and Attoh-Okine (2025) developed a joint model of multi-cycle geometric data and subgrade performance indicators, identifying spatial correlation degradation patterns that cannot be captured by the scalar TQI statistic—such as cyclic exceedances in specific mileage sections—and revealing the physical mechanism of localized support instability rather than global uniform degradation.
[p:87] The objective of analyzing ultrasonic testing signals is to extract information regarding the location, size, and type of defects from the echo sequence, thereby establishing a quantitative mapping from “signal anomalies” to “defect grades.” Phased Array Ultrasonic Testing (PAUT) uses electronic scanning to cover multiple incidence angles in a single excitation; its S/B/C scan images directly display the distribution of reflection amplitudes within the cross-section, providing a richer information source for defect geometric reconstruction than single-probe A-scan testing (Garcia and Zhang, 2006; Phrommahakul et al., 2025).
[p:88] Deep learning methods have significantly improved the efficiency of automated ultrasound image assessment(Islam and Olm, 2024; Lu et al., 2024). Related studies have demonstrated that the adoption of deep learning models enables the efficient and accurate identification of defects in track components in real-world scenarios, thereby helping to enhance the level of automated monitoring and maintenance of railway infrastructure.
[p:90] Vehicle-track dynamic models are widely used to analyze the response of the wheel-rail interface, axle boxes, or the vehicle body in order to assess track conditions and detect surface defects in the rails, or to analyze vertical and lateral wheel-rail forces based on the vehicle’s response (Yang et al., 2026). Early rail dynamics models were largely based on the theory of continuous elastic beams, simplifying the rail to an Euler-Bernoulli beam or a Timoshenko beam. These models combined a discrete elastic support model to simulate the track-bed support system and used analytical methods to solve for the coupled vibration response of heavy-haul vehicles and the track. For example, Chen et al. (2022) developed a vehicle-track coupled dynamic model capable of detecting track irregularities, ballast settlement, and fastener damage, thereby assessing the extent of track deterioration.
[p:92] However, purely data-driven methods for condition assessment and service life prediction generally suffer from insurmountable “black box” limitations. On the one hand, data models rely heavily on massive amounts of comprehensive, high-quality historical labeled data. In the complex and ever-changing operational environment of heavy-haul railways, where models must contend with severe noise interference, rare failures with small sample sizes, and unknown operating conditions, their generalization capabilities and robustness often face severe challenges. On the other hand, when performing feature extraction and trend inference, simple machine learning, deep neural networks, or large language models are completely detached from the inherent mechanical evolution mechanisms of the track system. This results in models that not only lack clear physical interpretability, but whose assessment decisions may even violate basic laws of dynamics and energy conservation. [p:95] From the perspective of technological evolution, one of the most significant changes in railway O&M lies in the transformation of data acquisition methods. Traditional maintenance mainly depends on manual patrols, periodic inspection vehicles, and field experience records, which often results in low-frequency, weakly structured, and fragmented data. These limitations make it difficult to support real-time diagnosis and long-term degradation prediction. In recent years, track inspection vehicles, onboard vibration sensors, wayside monitoring systems, machine vision, unmanned aerial vehicles, LiDAR, and Internet of Things devices have been increasingly applied to the monitoring of tracks, turnouts, bridges, tunnels, overhead contact systems, and rolling stock (Sedghi et al., 2021a). As a result, railway O&M data have become multi-source, heterogeneous, high-frequency, and strongly coupled in both space and time. More frequent, automated, and standardized inspection methods can improve the repeatability, consistency, and usability of maintenance data, thereby providing a foundation for machine learning and predictive maintenance (Bris-Peñalver, Verdecia-Peña and Alonso, 2026b).
[p:96] In terms of condition diagnosis and health assessment, machine learning and deep learning methods have been widely used in railway track geometry degradation prediction, rail defect detection, turnout fault diagnosis, rolling stock anomaly detection, and overhead contact system condition assessment (Bai et al., 2015; Falamarzi, Moridpour and Nazem, 2019). Existing studies generally focus on data sources such as track geometry measurements, vibration responses, and image-based inspection data. Commonly used methods include support vector machines, random forests, artificial neural networks, deep learning models, and genetic algorithms(Mohammadi and He, 2022; Rodríguez-Hernández et al., 2025a). Compared with traditional statistical models, data-driven methods are capable of extracting complex degradation patterns from historical data. However, they still face several challenges, including unstable data quality, imbalanced samples, insufficient model interpretability, and limited generalization ability across different lines and operating conditions(Bris-Peñalver, Verdecia-Peña and Alonso, 2026b). [p:99] In recent years, digital twin technology has been regarded as an important development direction for intelligent railway O&M. A digital twin can connect the physical railway system, real-time monitoring data, degradation models, and maintenance decision models, thereby providing a dynamic simulation environment for condition inference, fault warning, and maintenance scheme evaluation (Mutlu and Kaewunruen, 2026). Nevertheless, many current railway digital twin studies still remain at the level of digital representation or one-way monitoring platforms. A truly interactive, self-correcting, and closed-loop digital twin system has not yet been fully realized. In addition, although sustainability is frequently emphasized in railway maintenance studies, quantitative evaluation of green indicators such as carbon emissions, energy consumption, and environmental benefits remains insufficient.
[p:102] For long-distance railways, the key difficulty is that the asset is linear, geographically exposed, heterogeneous in track form and support condition, and strongly constrained by traffic windows. Network-level inspection can reveal where the state may be abnormal, but it often cannot determine the local physical cause, the exact repair boundary, or the expected repair effect. [p:110] The limitation of this body of research is not that it is too conservative, but that it often stops at threshold crossing and work-list generation. [p:113] Predictive maintenance in railway infrastructure is not a single algorithmic task. It is a data-to-decision pipeline that converts heterogeneous observations into condition states, forecasts, work priorities and intervention rules. Reviews in the railway domain consistently distinguish between defect detection, diagnosis or classification, prognosis, and maintenance-decision coupling (Davari et al., 2021; Binder, Mezhuyev and Tschandl, 2023; Bianchi et al., 2025c). In track applications, the data sources include geometry measurements, ultrasonic testing, visual inspection, GPR, wayside monitoring, axle-load records, maintenance logs, traffic history, environmental exposure, and manually written inspection reports. This heterogeneity is useful, but it creates practical problems: the data are collected at different sampling rates, spatial resolutions and coordinate systems, and the labels used for model training are often indirect operational labels rather than true physical failure labels.
[p:115] Methodologically, the literature can be grouped into four families. Machine-learning models, including random forests, support vector machines, neural networks and hybrid deep-learning architectures, have become more prominent as inspection datasets grow; (Y. Liao et al., 2022) review these models for track geometry degradation prediction. Physics-based and simulation-based models remain essential where labels are scarce but the vehicle-track mechanism is understood. [p:116] The most useful predictive outputs are not abstract accuracy scores. They are decision-ready quantities such as probability of threshold exceedance, time-to-limit, remaining useful life, defect recurrence risk, and prioritized work lists. (Caetano and Teixeira, 2016) show this principle in ballast tamping by linking degradation forecasting to a reliability-cost maintenance model. [p:119] The dominant state variable in track maintenance is geometry, but geometry is an effect rather than a complete cause. Geometry deterioration expresses how the rail-fastener-sleeper-support system evolves under repeated wheel loading, environmental exposure and previous interventions. In ballasted track, the central mechanism is the gradual loss of uniform support. Ballast settlement, particle breakage, abrasion, fouling, drainage degradation and subgrade weakening change the stiffness and damping of the track bed, leading to longitudinal-level and alignment irregularities. In this setting, tamping remains the main corrective operation because it directly restores sleeper support and rail position. Yet the recovery is imperfect and temporary. (Audley and Andrews, 2013) found that traffic speed and maintenance history affect subsequent deterioration and that tamping can damage ballast. This is why modern maintenance models treat tamping not as a reset to an as-new state, but as an intervention with limited and path-dependent recovery.
[p:121] Rail and wheel-rail interface deterioration add another mechanism. Wear, corrugation, rolling contact fatigue, squats, head checks and profile evolution affect contact stress, dynamic loading, noise and vibration. Rail grinding and profile maintenance are therefore not only surface operations; they regulate the dynamic interaction that drives downstream track damage. Numerical wear-model reviews describe a standard chain in which multibody vehicle-track dynamics generates contact forces and creepages, contact mechanics resolves stresses, a wear law estimates material loss, and iterative profile updating predicts future profile evolution (Bosso, Magelli and Zampieri, 2022). Corrugation assessment based on wheel-rail forces and rail-grinding needs has also been studied as a way to connect measurement to grinding decisions (Niu et al., 2023).
[p:122] Transition zones and discrete assets are another major source of state evolution. Bridges, culverts, tunnels, level crossings, turnouts and slab-ballast interfaces generate abrupt changes in stiffness, support condition or geometry. The resulting dynamic amplification produces differential settlement, local geometry loss and recurring maintenance demand. (Shang et al., 2021) argue that discrete rail assets require systems-thinking maintenance because they are short, numerous, heterogeneous and locally degraded, while (Shang et al., 2023) show how level crossings and associated transition zones can be optimized through surrogate-assisted dynamic design. The message for maintenance is clear: recurring defects should not be treated only by repeated geometry correction. Effective state-evolution management must identify whether the root cause lies in ballast fouling, sleeper support, rail profile, slab interface, drainage, stiffness transition or component failure.
[p:126] Recent autonomous railway platforms indicate a transition from inspection-centered automation toward execution-oriented maintenance. (J. Li et al., 2024) present a Railway Inspection and Maintenance Vehicle that integrates GPS, IMU, wheel encoders, LiDAR, depth camera, environmental camera, ROS2-based processing, remote monitoring, and emergency stop mechanisms. Although the reported validation mainly focuses on ultrasonic inspection, the system is important because it provides a modular mobile carrier for railway tasks. In other words, its value is not only defect sensing, but also the possibility of carrying different payloads, navigating along the rail corridor, and linking inspection data with field action. Such platforms provide the hardware basis for future automated intervention, particularly when local measurement, positioning, and task-specific tools can be integrated into the same vehicle.
[p:127] Task-specific maintenance robots provide a more direct example of automated execution. (Guo, Zhang and Qi, 2019) develop a rail-fastener installation robot that combines visual recognition, sensor-based bolt localization, automatic feeding, manipulator control, and electric-wrench fastening under PLC control. This system is narrow in task scope, but it is highly relevant for precision maintenance because it demonstrates the full chain from perception to positioning and physical action. Fastener work is also a representative case of railway maintenance automation: the task is repetitive, spatially regular, safety-critical, and requires controlled torque or fastening quality. This makes it suitable for early robotic deployment before broader, less structured maintenance tasks can be automated.
[p:128] Mobile manipulation is therefore a key bridge between inspection platforms and real repair robots. (Rahman et al., 2023) note that railway inspection technologies are more mature than robotic repair technologies, and that mobile manipulators are still rarely deployed in railway track maintenance. The difficulty lies not only in mechanical design but also in the railway working environment. Maintenance robots must operate under vibration, ballast unevenness, limited clearance, weather exposure, electromagnetic interference, strict safety requirements, and tight possession windows. They must also handle tools, materials, fasteners, or rail components with reliable localization and quality assurance. For this reason, future robotic maintenance is more likely to begin with constrained, task-specific operations rather than fully general autonomous repair.
[p:129] Human-in-the-loop automation is another important path. Augmented reality and digital guidance technologies can support maintenance by providing visual instructions, spatial registration, and diagnostic guidance to operators. (Koh et al., 2020) show that AR-based maintenance for robots commonly relies on tracking, registration, and visual guidance, although many applications remain laboratory-based. In railway maintenance, such methods may be useful as transitional technologies. Before full robotic autonomy becomes reliable, AR-assisted execution can improve field consistency, reduce training demand, and connect human repair actions with digital work orders and inspection records. It also supports supervised automation, where a human operator monitors or confirms robotic action rather than performing the entire task manually.
[p:130] Embodied AI provides the conceptual basis for more advanced robotic maintenance. (Sun et al., 2024) describe embodied intelligence as the integration of perception, cognition, and behavior through physical interaction with the environment. (Zheng et al., 2025) similarly organize embodied AI around perception, decision-making, and execution. These frameworks are relevant because railway maintenance robots cannot be treated as simple actuators. They must perceive the local track state, interpret the repair target, select an action strategy, execute it physically, and evaluate the resulting change. Object-centric robotic manipulation research further shows that reliable execution depends on pose estimation, affordance learning, policy learning, and task-oriented manipulation (Zheng et al., 2025). These capabilities are directly relevant to fine maintenance actions such as fastening, tool positioning, component replacement, surface treatment, and local adjustment.
[p:131] Overall, the current literature suggests that railway maintenance automation is moving toward human-supervised, task-constrained, and verification-based robotics. The immediate goal is not unrestricted autonomy, but the formation of executable maintenance units: a robot or robotic platform should be able to reach the target site, localize the defect, perform a specific repair, measure the result, and report whether the intervention satisfies acceptance criteria. This execution logic is closely aligned with the second closed loop proposed in this review, where precision maintenance is realized through repeated measurement, decision, control, and re-measurement rather than through one-off repair.

[p:142] Sensing Technology Enhancement
[p:143] To address the inherent limitations of intermittent inspections by specialized Track Geometry Cars (TGCs), which often fail to capture the rapid deterioration of track quality in high-density railway networks, the first loop of the intelligent O&M system introduces a continuous general survey technology based on operational trains. A fundamental prerequisite for this technology is the utilization of vehicles operating under constant speed, constant load, and consistent operational states (referred to as "isostates"). These isostates minimize the non-linear uncertainties in vehicle-track coupled dynamics, providing a stable "baseline" for the long-term trend analysis of track health. This sensing enhancement is realized through a transition from isolated sensor measurements to a multi-dimensional, pervasive perception framework that integrates hierarchical vibration, inertial references, and acoustic signals.
[p:144] The core of this architecture is the multi-level vibration sensing system, which utilizes the vehicle’s mechanical structure as a natural transducer. Different vehicle components exhibit distinct filtering characteristics that correlate with specific track irregularity wavelengths. Axle Box Acceleration is directly coupled with the wheel-rail interface, making it exceptionally sensitive to high-frequency impacts. It is the primary data source for diagnosing rail surface defects and short-wavelength irregularities, such as squats, insulated joint failures, and rail corrugation(Molodova, Li and Dollevoet, 2011; Salvador et al., 2016) . However, the extreme vibration environment of the axle box often introduces high-frequency noise and integration drift, which can obscure geometric trends(Real Herráiz et al., 2012). To bridge this gap, bogie and car body accelerations are integrated into the system. While the primary and secondary suspension systems filter out high-frequency content, these sensors provide high-fidelity data for medium-to-long wavelength irregularities . Such data is critical for monitoring large-scale infrastructure changes, including subgrade settlement, bridge pier 
[p:148] To further enhance the precision of spatial trajectory reconstruction, Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) are incorporated to provide high-precision angular velocity data (e.g., pitch, roll, and yaw rates). Pure acceleration-based methods often suffer from signal-to-noise ratio degradation at low frequencies or during low-speed operations. The introduction of gyroscopic sensors allows for the compensation of these low-frequency errors. For instance, the bogie yaw rate has been demonstrated to be significantly more robust than lateral acceleration for estimating track alignment and gauge-related irregularities when the train operates at velocities as low as 10 m/s (Weston et al., 2007). By applying complementary filtering or Kalman filtering to fuse acceleration and angular velocity, the sensing system can accurately decouple the vehicle's attitude from the track's true geometry, enabling a precise mapping of the "absolute" track profile across a broad spectrum of wavelengths (Escalona, 2016).
[p:149] Lastly, the fusion of acoustic sensing adds a non-contact diagnostic dimension to the general survey. Acoustic signals, such as wheel-rail interaction noise and interior carriage noise, possess extremely high sampling rates, allowing for the identification of micro-scale surface degradations that may not yet have triggered significant vibration responses. Advanced signal processing techniques, such as wavelet packet energy analysis and 1D-CNN-based feature extraction, have enabled the quantification of rail corrugation and wheel flats with high accuracy (Wei et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2022). This synergy of multi-level vibration, inertial navigation, and acoustic perception creates a robust, "all-weather" sensing layer, ensuring that the continuous general survey can provide comprehensive and reliable track state information for subsequent AI-driven assessment.
[p:150] Data Imputation and Enhancement
[p:151] Within the framework of continuous general survey technology based on on-board vibration-noise sensing, the efficacy of downstream diagnostic models is fundamentally bottlenecked by raw data quality. Despite the deployment of advanced sensor networks across large-scale railway infrastructures, continuous signal acquisition is inevitably compromised by harsh dynamic environments, transient communication latencies, and sensor degradation. This disruption leads to highly fragmented and intrinsically imbalanced datasets. Consequently, establishing a robust data preprocessing pipeline is a critical prerequisite for reliable intelligent operation and maintenance (O&M). This pipeline specifically encompasses data imputation to rectify missing sensor observations and data enhancement to amplify rare fault signatures. The evolution of these methodologies reflects a continuous drive to capture the complex spatiotemporal dynamics of railway systems.
[p:152] Under Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) or Missing at Random (MAR) assumptions, early data imputation typically relied on simple mean substitution or linear interpolation. To mitigate the bias of these univariate methods, multivariate statistical frameworks like Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) (Buuren and Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011) were widely adopted. In transportation domains, foundational machine learning algorithms have demonstrated significant efficacy; for instance, Probabilistic Principal Component Analysis (PPCA) effectively recovers missing traffic flow data by extracting linear feature correlations (Qu et al., 2009). Additionally, the non-parametric MissForest algorithm (Stekhoven and Bühlmann, 2012) excels in handling mixed-type data without strict distributional assumptions. However, these fundamental approaches inherently rely on linear or local proximity assumptions, rendering them inadequate for capturing the highly non-linear, multidimensional dependencies within complex railway sensor networks.
[p:153] To address these multidimensional dependencies, matrix factorization initially emerged to reconstruct missing values by extracting latent low-rank structures from highly sparse datasets(Koren, Bell and Volinsky, 2009). Because intelligent transportation data naturally encompass complex spatiotemporal dimensions—such as spatial nodes, time intervals, and sensor features—they inherently form multidimensional tensors rather than two-dimensional matrices. Consequently, low-rank tensor factorization extends this paradigm to preserve global topological properties across multiple modes, effectively capturing multi-way correlations for missing traffic volume imputation(Giancarlo, 2018). Nevertheless, their reliance on static rank assumptions restricts their adaptability to highly non-linear spatiotemporal shifts.
[p:155] To overcome the constraints of discrete time steps, Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (Neural ODEs) offer a paradigm shift by modeling latent states as continuous-time dynamics. This continuous formulation is particularly adept at handling irregularly sampled data caused by transient communication failures in high-speed railways. Latent ODEs pioneered this approach by naturally interpolating missing values without requiring artificial time alignment(Rubanova, Chen and Duvenaud, 2019). Furthermore, Neural Controlled Differential Equations (NCDEs) extended this capability to process continuous incoming data streams (Kidger et al., 2020). In intelligent transportation, integrating continuous temporal CDEs with spatial graph networks has successfully resolved irregular traffic sensor failures(Choi et al., 2021). While highly effective for continuous temporal modeling, these dynamic systems do not explicitly capture the complex, underlying probability distributions of missing values, necessitating the introduction of deep generative frameworks.
[p:156] To explicitly capture these complex underlying probability distributions, deep generative frameworks—most notably Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)—have been widely adopted. Unlike deterministic systems, generative approaches reconstruct missing values by learning the non-linear data distribution within a latent space. The pioneering Generative Adversarial Imputation Nets (GAIN) introduced an adversarial mechanism where a generator imputes missing components while a discriminator distinguishes them from observed data(Yoon, Jordon and Schaar, 2018). Within railway condition monitoring, customized GAN architectures have been successfully deployed to recover missing multi-sensor data from rolling stock and mechanical bearings, significantly enhancing diagnostic reliability(X. Wang et al., 2025). However, GAN-based imputation frequently suffers from training instability and mode collapse, highlighting the critical need for more stable and tractable probabilistic generative paradigms.
[p:157] To address the instability of adversarial training, diffusion models have recently emerged as the state-of-the-art probabilistic paradigm for data imputation. By defining a forward noise-injection process and learning a parameterized reverse denoising trajectory, these models offer highly stable and tractable generation. Conditional Score-based Diffusion Models (CSDI) pioneered this approach by probabilistically imputing time-series data through a conditional denoising mechanism(Tashiro et al., 2021). This process is particularly advantageous for complex, high-dimensional transportation networks. Recently, conditional diffusion frameworks have been tailored to reconstruct missing continuous multi-sensor signals in high-speed trains, demonstrating superior distributional fidelity compared to previous baselines(Yang et al., 2025). While these advanced imputation techniques successfully restore data completeness, the inherent scarcity of critical fault samples in real-world railway operations necessitates the complementary integration of data enhancement strategies.
[p:158] While high-fidelity data imputation is critical for restoring the continuity of sensor networks, data enhancement is equally indispensable for the intelligent operation and maintenance of large-scale railway infrastructures. Imputation methodologies primarily address the random missingness of observations but cannot overcome the inherent scarcity and severe class imbalance of critical fault events. In real-world railway operations, anomalous conditions occur infrequently, resulting in a pronounced long-tail distribution. Subsequently, the Adaptive Synthetic (ADASYN) approach dynamically generates more data for harder-to-learn minority examples, adaptively shifting the decision boundary(He et al., 2008). [p:159] To overcome linear interpolation artifacts, signal-level transformations and feature mixing strategies directly manipulate continuous time-series data to simulate diverse operating conditions. Techniques such as adding Gaussian noise or time-stretching physically alter original sensor signals, while the mix-up approach improves model generalization by interpolating both features and labels to smooth decision boundaries(Zhang et al., 2018). Within railway condition monitoring, these model-independent augmentations have proven highly effective for diagnosing faults in critical rotating machinery under variable dynamic loads(Lyu et al., 2022). Nevertheless, because signal warping and feature mixing primarily recombine existing observations rather than generating entirely new, high-fidelity fault distributions, capturing the highly complex non-linearities of rare railway defects necessitates a transition to advanced deep generative modeling.
[p:160] To synthesize entirely new, high-fidelity fault distributions, Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have become the cornerstone of advanced data enhancement. By optimizing an adversarial mechanism, conditional GANs, such as CTGAN, effectively model non-Gaussian and discrete data without relying on prior structural assumptions(Xu et al., 2019). In railway engineering, customized GANs are increasingly deployed to synthesize realistic samples of extreme minority classes, specifically generating artificial railway track irregularities to augment sparse diagnostic datasets(Bobadilla and Martin, 2024). However, while standard GANs excel given moderate sample sizes, they frequently suffer from mode collapse and degraded feature boundaries when trained under extremely limited data regimes, driving the adoption of contrastive learning and diffusion models.
[p:161] To overcome mode collapse in extremely limited data regimes, contrastive learning and diffusion architectures have been introduced to stabilize feature synthesis. Contrastive learning enhances generative networks by explicitly maximizing latent distances between distinct classes, thereby sharpening decision boundaries. In railway applications, contrastive GANs have successfully synthesized rare rail surface defects, preventing training instability when real samples are exceptionally scarce(Du, Gao and Li, 2023). Concurrently, diffusion models have transitioned from data imputation to augmentation. Frameworks such as TabDDPM (Kotelnikov et al., 2024) synthesize high-fidelity structural data through stable iterative denoising, outperforming standard GANs with minimal prior observations. Nevertheless, applying these highly complex probabilistic models directly to extensive, multivariate railway sensor signals necessitates their integration with advanced sequential representation architectures.
[p:162] To effectively process extensive, multivariate sensor sequences, Transformer-based architectures have redefined data enhancement paradigms. By leveraging self-attention mechanisms, Transformers capture long-range temporal dependencies without relying on localized receptive fields. To enhance sequence representations under limited supervision, frameworks have been designed to intentionally mask and reconstruct segments of multivariate time series, thereby extracting robust generalized features(Zerveas et al., 2020). In intelligent railway operation and maintenance, this paradigm elegantly addresses severe fault sample shortages. Notably, recent approaches integrating Convolutional Transformers with pseudo-synthetic data generation algorithms have significantly improved the fault diagnosis accuracy of complex railway turnout systems(Fu et al., 2024). Ultimately, these attention-driven enhancement strategies provide the requisite high-dimensional feature fidelity to support robust, large-scale predictive maintenance.
[p:163] In summary, the evolution of data imputation and enhancement strategies provides a comprehensive solution to the inherent data imperfections of on-board vibration-noise sensing systems. The progression from foundational statistical interpolations to state-of-the-art continuous dynamics and generative architectures ensures the extraction of high-fidelity, balanced feature spaces from chaotic sensor streams. By strictly reconstructing missing temporal dynamics and artificially synthesizing minority fault representations, these advanced methodologies bridge the critical gap between raw hardware perception and actionable intelligence. Ultimately, this stabilized and enriched data foundation paves the way for the subsequent deployment of advanced AI-based inverse mapping models and large-scale assessment frameworks in railway predictive maintenance.

[p:170] At the same time, the most significant structural limitation of the current generation of inverse mapping models is their implicit assumption of stationarity. Every supervised learning model implicitly encodes a prior distribution regarding the joint distribution of input signals and output track states observed during training. When a model trained on summer data from flat terrain is deployed on a winter mountain slope line, the mechanics of wheel-rail contact, the thermal behavior of ballast, and the aerodynamic load spectrum will all shift simultaneously—and the trained mapping model will confuse track conditions with these operational effects, losing calibration accuracy in a systematic manner that is difficult to detect in the absence of ground truth. This issue is not marginal but fundamental: the entire value proposition of on-board continuous sensing hinges on the ability of models calibrated during dedicated measurement periods to generalize to daily passenger operations under all conditions.
[p:171] A promising research direction to address this challenge lies in domain-adversarial training frameworks, which explicitly decouple track-state-dependent features from operational-condition-dependent confounding factors, training models to generate representations capable of predicting track states while maintaining invariance to speed, vehicle load, and seasonal factors. Complementing this, meta-learning approaches—which train models on a task distribution rather than a single task—offer the potential for rapid, low-data-requirement adaptation to new operational conditions or previously unencountered track geometries, thereby significantly reducing the need for re-calibration data when deploying models to new network segments. The construction of standardized, cross-network benchmark datasets capable of capturing the full diversity of operational conditions across multiple railway systems will serve as an indispensable foundational project for advancing and fairly evaluating the aforementioned adaptive strategies.
[p:173] PINN offers a structurally distinct and theoretically grounded approach to simultaneously address the aforementioned challenges. [p:174] This framework possesses several properties directly relevant to operations: physical residuals serve as a powerful regularization mechanism under sparse sensor conditions, enabling reliable parameter identification from data with a measurement density far lower than that required by purely data-driven methods; the recovered parameters can be interpreted directly as physical quantities rather than abstract latent variables; physical constraints implicitly encode prior knowledge about structural behavior, partially mitigating distribution shifts by anchoring the model to physical laws that hold regardless of operational conditions (Haghighat et al., 2021; Xu and Darve, 2021).

[p:193] The second closed loop should be understood as a precision-maintenance loop rather than as a general robotics loop. In the proposed dual-loop architecture(Figure 2), the first loop supplies broad coverage: it observes long mileage, detects abnormal state evolution, ranks risk, and identifies candidate work sites. The second loop is activated only after such a candidate has been identified. Its purpose is not to inspect the whole network again, but to transform a warning into a local repair target, execute an intervention with controllable parameters, and verify the effect through remeasurement. This is the conceptual shift from “knowing that a section is deteriorating” to “knowing exactly what to repair, how to repair it, and whether the repair has changed the state as expected.”
[p:196] Four design principles follow. First, the loop must be target-specific: each task should define its own measurable state, action variable and acceptance criterion. Second, the loop must be coordinate-consistent: pre-repair measurement, execution and post-repair verification must refer to the same mileage and asset object. Third, the loop must be effect-oriented: success should be judged by state change and durability, not merely by work completion. Fourth, the loop must be incrementally deployable: early systems should focus on bounded tasks such as rail-profile correction, fastener intervention, local geometry restoration or ballast-condition verification before moving toward broader autonomous repair.
[p:198] The first step of the second loop is to convert a network-level alert into a repairable engineering object. A first-loop model may detect abnormal vibration, noise, track geometry, rail corrugation, stiffness variation, fastener anomaly or state-evolution rate over a relatively broad interval. Such an output is valuable for screening, but it is not yet a repair instruction. A repair instruction requires a much tighter definition: the target asset, the mileage position, the defect boundary, the dominant mechanism, the intervention type, the action parameters and the acceptance test. The second loop therefore begins at the point where condition monitoring becomes repair specification.
[p:200] The conversion also requires diagnostic precision. Many railway defects are symptoms of deeper mechanisms. A local irregularity may result from ballast fouling, drainage failure, hanging sleepers, transition-zone stiffness mismatch, subgrade frost heave or slab-interface voiding. Repeatedly correcting the visible geometry without addressing the cause may improve short-term measurement values while increasing long-term recurrence. In the second loop, the target object should therefore include a causal hypothesis. The hypothesis need not be perfect, but it must be explicit enough to guide the choice between tamping, grinding, cleaning, tightening, replacement, grouting, drainage improvement or structural reinforcement.
[p:201] Finally, the conversion requires action precision. A repair category is not sufficient. Tamping requires lift and lining values; grinding requires depth, pass pattern and profile target; fastener intervention requires torque or replacement criteria; ballast cleaning requires depth and boundary; slab repair requires injection or local structural treatment parameters. The second loop becomes meaningful only when the system can connect the measured state to such controllable action variables. This is the main difference between precision maintenance and enhanced diagnosis.
[p:204] A practical second-loop measurement system should combine three levels. The first is high-resolution surface measurement, including track geometry, rail profile, corrugation, surface defect and component-position sensing. This level defines the visible target and the required boundary of action. The second is support-condition or subsurface diagnosis, such as ballast fouling, voids, moisture or weak support zones. This level helps distinguish symptoms from causes. The third is dynamic or contextual measurement, including acceleration, strain, temperature, axle-load context and local response under operating conditions. This level is especially important at transition zones, where measured geometry alone may underestimate the mechanism that causes repeated deterioration.
[p:205] The central algorithmic problem is not only feature extraction but mileage alignment. The same target must be recoverable across the first-loop alert, local precision measurement, tool execution and post-repair remeasurement. This requires fusion of odometry, inertial sensing, GNSS or BeiDou when available, known track features, digital track maps, signal matching and machine-specific offsets. The issue is often treated as a preprocessing step, but in precision maintenance it is a core safety function. If before-after data cannot be proven to refer to the same object, the system cannot claim that the intervention changed the state.
[p:208] The decision module of the second loop should be local, real-time and intervention-oriented. Its role is not to generate an annual maintenance plan; that remains the task of the maintenance-management system and the first loop. The second-loop decision module answers immediate questions: what is the dominant maintenance object, what action should be taken, what parameters should be used, what risk should be controlled during execution, and what measured result will count as successful repair. This decision is narrower than network optimization but technically more demanding because it is tied to a physical operation.
[p:210] The action model should be expressed as a state-action-effect tuple. The state contains the local features and their uncertainty; the action contains the repair type and process parameters; the effect contains the target before-after change and the expected durability. This tuple is useful for two reasons. Operationally, it tells the machine or crew what to do and how to verify it. Scientifically, it creates a rare but valuable dataset: pre-repair state, executed action, process log, post-repair state and later recurrence. Such paired intervention data are more useful for future learning than isolated inspection data because they record how the infrastructure responds to maintenance.
[p:211] Controllable intervention requires process feedback. For grinding, this may include actual removal depth, post-profile error and thermal or surface-quality constraints. For tamping, it may include lift, lining, compaction and residual geometry deviation. For fastener work, it may include target localization, torque, tool angle and visual confirmation. For ballast or slab support treatment, it may include injected volume, pressure response, subsurface change and local geometry recovery. The common requirement is that the machine should not only receive a command; it should report what was actually delivered. This delivery record is the bridge between decision and verification.
[p:212] The second loop also changes the meaning of optimization. In conventional planning, optimization often means minimizing cost, possession time or expected risk at the network level. In precision maintenance, optimization is local and effect-based: choose the smallest safe intervention that changes the state sufficiently and durably. This principle is important because over-intervention may waste possession time and damage materials, while under-intervention may produce rapid recurrence. The second loop should therefore learn not only where to intervene, but also how much intervention is enough.
[p:217] Remeasurement is the closure condition of the second loop. After the intervention, the system must measure the same target again using the same coordinate reference or a calibrated equivalent. The remeasurement should answer four questions: was the target reached, did the state change as intended, does the residual state satisfy the acceptance criterion, and should another local correction be performed? This makes maintenance evidence-based at the action level. A conventional work record may show that a grinding, tamping or fastening job was completed. A closed-loop record shows the pre-repair state, the selected action, the delivered process parameters, the post-repair state and the decision about acceptance or rework.
[p:219] In this sense, the second loop is the mechanism that turns state evolution into state control. The first loop tells the operator where the infrastructure is changing and where risk may accumulate. The second loop intervenes at selected sites, measures the local object in higher resolution, chooses a controllable action, verifies the effect and records the result. Over time, this architecture should reduce repeated symptom treatment, improve the traceability of maintenance decisions, and create a high-quality intervention dataset. Its value is not only automation; it is the ability to make railway maintenance more precise, explainable and learnable.

Consequently, cross-line transfer should be combined with uncertainty estimation and a mechanism for requesting target-line labels when model confidence is insufficient.

The proposed condition indicator was calculated from track responses in a wavelength-related frequency band and exhibited a strong relationship with simulated vertical wheel-rail contact force under different crossing and track conditions. This case is significant because it links a measurable dynamic response to a physically meaningful interaction quantity. It therefore provides more diagnostic value than an isolated acceleration threshold.
[p:233] The measured-simulated comparison also exposes the limitations of the current implementation. The scanned crossing regions were shorter than the full dynamic interaction zone, and measured accelerations contained excitation sources not represented in the model, including long-wavelength irregularities, wheel out-of-roundness and conditions outside the scanned area. Calibration was more successful for sleeper displacement than for velocity and acceleration. These findings are not weaknesses to be hidden; they illustrate the role of open-loop uncertainty in real railway applications. A physics-assisted monitoring system must continuously update its boundary conditions and quantify the discrepancy between the model and the measured asset before its indicator can be used to trigger maintenance automatically.
[p:234] The studies by Rahman et al. (2024), Ghiasi et al. (2025) and Milosevic et al. (2022) can thus be read as a connected sequence. DAS expands temporal and spatial coverage, drive-by monitoring uses operational vehicles to acquire repeated line-scale responses, domain adaptation supports knowledge transfer between lines, and asset-specific sensing supplies local diagnostic resolution. Together they define a multi-scale perception architecture. The unresolved issue is how to coordinate these levels so that a weak corridor-scale anomaly can initiate targeted inspection, obtain a mechanism-oriented diagnosis and produce an executable work order.
[p:237] Sresakoolchai and Kaewunruen (2022) demonstrated the first stage of this progression by combining track geometry measurements, component-defect records and track-profile data. Their case covered a 30-km section measured at one-foot intervals over four years from 2016 to 2019. Supervised models were used to identify rail, rail-joint, fastener and switch-and-crossing defects, while clustering and association-rule analysis were used to explore relationships among defect types and track characteristics. The best-performing deep neural network achieved approximately 94% accuracy. More important than the individual accuracy value is the change in the function of the data: geometry records collected for quality assessment were reused to infer component-level conditions that would otherwise require additional manual inspection.
[p:238] This form of data reuse can increase the value of existing inspection systems without requiring immediate replacement of the sensing platform. However, it also shifts the bottleneck toward data governance. Component-defect labels must be spatially synchronized with geometry records, and differences in inspection timing can introduce false associations. Rare but safety-critical defects may be underrepresented, while high aggregate accuracy can conceal poor sensitivity for individual classes. The model output should therefore be interpreted as diagnostic evidence for targeted reinspection rather than as an autonomous maintenance command.
[p:239] Wang, Liu and Euston (2023) extended geometry-based interpretation further by integrating track geometry defects with substructure and operational information on a passenger railroad in the United States. Their framework combined GPR-derived ballast-condition variables, rail-seat abrasion, traffic density, track class, curvature and maintenance-related information to estimate the risk of geometry-defect occurrence. This case changes the diagnostic question from "which component defect accompanies the measured geometry?" to "which underlying support and operating conditions make geometry defects more likely?" It therefore represents a transition from component classification to hidden-state and risk interpretation.
[p:242] The integration framework in Wang et al. is particularly relevant to the proposed first loop because it shows that data fusion is not merely the concatenation of sensor channels. Geometry, GPR, rail-seat abrasion, traffic and track-class data possess different spatial resolutions and reference systems. They must be segmented, registered and converted into consistent features before a data-driven risk model can be established. The case thus provides an engineering counterpart to the multi-source fusion concept discussed earlier in this paper.
[p:243] The residual limitation is causal ambiguity. A geometry defect may result from ballast fouling, local stiffness variation, rail-seat abrasion, drainage failure or interacting causes. A predictive model can identify statistical risk without proving a unique deterioration mechanism. Consequently, its output should be used to guide additional inspection and maintenance prioritization. The precision intervention loop requires one further step: converting a high-risk section into a justified treatment type, such as tamping, ballast cleaning, drainage improvement, sleeper treatment or subgrade reinforcement.
[p:244] Environmental cases broaden the concept of hidden state from structural condition to external forcing. Roghani (2021) investigated a frost-susceptible track section over the winter-spring periods of 2019 and 2020 using thermistors, total-station surveys and track geometry measurements. The observations linked freezing-degree days, frost penetration, track-surface deformation and changes in profile and alignment roughness. The study showed that freeze-thaw cycling can alter both the magnitude and spatial distribution of track deformation, particularly near geotechnically sensitive features such as culverts.

[p:247] This field evidence has two implications for state-evolution modelling. First, deterioration cannot always be represented by a stationary curve derived only from historical geometry measurements. The evolution law changes when moisture, frost depth and temperature cycles change. Second, prediction confidence should vary with environmental conditions. [p:249] The above cases form a coherent assessment chain. Geometry data can first be used to infer component defects, then integrated with substructure and operational variables to estimate deterioration risk, and finally interpreted within changing environmental boundary conditions. This progression is central to the "state evolution" philosophy: the objective is not only to describe the current track geometry, but to determine which mechanism is active, how confidently it has been identified and how the condition may evolve under future traffic and environmental loading.
[p:251] The output of state assessment has operational value only when it changes maintenance action. This requires a transition from diagnosis to timing, treatment selection and resource allocation. It also requires post-intervention monitoring, because an intelligent O&M system must learn whether the selected treatment changed the subsequent deterioration trajectory.
[p:255] Within the first loop, the contribution of this analysis is to convert "earlier detection" into a constrained maintenance decision. Continuous sensing has a cost, false alarms create unnecessary intervention, and premature maintenance can consume resources without producing proportional performance gains. Conversely, delayed intervention can accelerate deterioration and increase renewal costs. Predictive maintenance should therefore optimize the expected long-term outcome rather than minimize a single condition indicator. In practical deployment, the economic model must be connected with line availability, maintenance-window constraints, safety risk and the measured effectiveness of alternative treatments.
[p:257] This case is more than an example of a reinforcement material. In the dual-loop context, it illustrates how a physical intervention can be evaluated by its influence on the future state trajectory. The maintenance action is not treated as the end of the process. Instead, subsequent geometry measurements are used to compare deterioration phases, traffic accumulation and the performance of treated and untreated sections. This is the type of feedback required to update deterioration models and future treatment selection.
[p:258] The field evidence also demonstrates why intervention assessment is difficult. Drainage condition, water pockets, precipitation, ballast screening, traffic accumulation and repeated tamping all influence the measured geometry. A simple before-and-after comparison may therefore attribute too much of the observed change to the intervention itself. A closed-loop system should maintain a structured record of treatment type, location, date, environmental condition, traffic exposure and post-maintenance response. Such records would allow the maintenance model to distinguish immediate geometry recovery from long-term deterioration reduction.
[p:259] Praticò and Fedele (2025) and Fischer (2022) together connect the two sides of the second loop. The former asks when predictive maintenance is economically justified, whereas the latter shows how the long-term effect of a physical intervention can be measured. The remaining integration task is to connect predicted risk and cost-benefit analysis to a specific executable treatment and then return the measured outcome to the model. Without this feedback, predictive maintenance remains a planning concept rather than a learning closed loop.
